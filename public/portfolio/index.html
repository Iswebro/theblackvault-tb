Digital miners, without the data-centre headache

Traditional Bitcoin mining asks a lot before it gives anything back: specialised hardware, a suitable power supply, cooling, maintenance, noise management, and the patience to keep every moving part online. GoMining changes the interface. Its digital miners are backed by real computing power operating in physical data centres, while the user manages the experience through an app.

The idea is simple enough to explain in one sentence: a digital miner gives you access to hosted Bitcoin mining power without asking you to run the hardware yourself. The useful part comes next, because not every miner is identical.

Two numbers tell most of the story

TH, or terahashes per second, is the miner’s computing power. More TH means a larger contribution to mining activity. It is the output side of the equation.

W/TH describes energy efficiency. It shows how much electricity is needed for each unit of computing power. Lower W/TH means the miner uses less energy for the same amount of hashrate, which can improve its cost efficiency.

Put them together and the product becomes easier to compare: TH tells you how much work the miner can do; W/TH helps show how efficiently it does that work. Power attracts attention, but efficiency helps explain the net result.

Choose the experience, not only the asset

A digital miner can be used in different ways inside the GoMining ecosystem. In Mining Mode, users receive BTC rewards based on the mining power and efficiency attached to their miners, after applicable operating costs. In Miner Wars, miners become part of a competitive game where players join clans, use strategy, and compete for BTC and GOMINING rewards.

That choice matters because the same product can serve two different motivations. One user may prefer a more passive routine. Another may enjoy the timing, teamwork, and competition of a game. Clear product copy should make the difference visible before asking for a click.

What clear copy should never hide

Mining rewards are not a fixed promise. Bitcoin network conditions, mining difficulty, the miner’s specifications, operating costs, and product rules can all affect outcomes. An estimator can help a user model a scenario, but it is still an estimate.

That is not a reason to bury the product under disclaimers. It is a reason to write with precision. Explain what the user controls, what the network controls, and what can change. Trust grows when the copy answers the second question before the reader needs to ask it.

The clearest next step

Start by comparing power and efficiency. Then decide whether you want the rhythm of Mining Mode or the strategy of Miner Wars. The machinery may be running elsewhere, but the decision should still feel completely visible.

From support friction to calm next steps

An anonymised support-writing example showing how empathy, security, and one clear action can turn a tense conversation into useful guidance.

See the before-and-after
Before
Your account issue cannot be handled here. Contact support and wait. Do not message moderators.
After
Thanks for flagging this. Moderators cannot access or change account data, but the support team can review it securely. Please open in-app support and include your account email, a screenshot of the error, and the time it happened. Do not share your seed phrase, private key, password, or verification codes with anyone in chat.

Why the edit works

  • Starts by acknowledging the user instead of rejecting them.
  • Explains the moderator boundary, so the handoff feels logical rather than dismissive.
  • Names the exact evidence support needs, reducing a second round of questions.
  • Adds a security warning at the moment a stressed user is most vulnerable to impersonation.

Short-form anti-scam version

⚠️ That direct message is not an official support process. Do not connect your wallet, send funds, or share a seed phrase, private key, password, or verification code. Block and report the account, then contact support through the app or the verified support channel.

AI moves the first draft. Judgment moves the work.

A miniature case study showing how a structured brief and a ruthless edit turn generic AI language into useful product copy.

Review the editorial case study

The brief

Explain TH and W/TH to a first-time digital-miner buyer in no more than 70 words. Use plain English. Give each metric a different job. Do not promise returns. End with a decision-oriented takeaway.
First AI draft
Hashrate and energy efficiency are two important metrics to consider when evaluating a digital miner. TH/s represents computational performance, while W/TH indicates electricity consumption relative to computing power. Higher hashrate and lower energy consumption may provide optimised mining performance and potentially improve profitability.
Human-edited final
TH tells you how much mining power the miner has. W/TH tells you how much energy that power needs.

More TH increases the miner’s contribution. Lower W/TH means better energy efficiency, which can reduce operating costs. Compare both: power shows the output; efficiency helps explain the net result.

Editorial decisions

  • Removed abstract phrases such as “computational performance” and “optimised”.
  • Gave each metric a separate, memorable role.
  • Replaced “profitability” with a more precise reference to operating costs and net results.
  • Cut the hedge-filled ending and replaced it with a practical comparison rule.

My working framework

Use: factual scaffolding, variants, outlines, compression, tone comparison, blind-spot checks.

Rewrite: vague claims, flat rhythm, borrowed tone, repeated structures, unsupported conclusions.

Reject: invented facts, false certainty, compliance shortcuts, and copy that cannot explain its own purpose.

Approach

Fast does not mean careless.

AI can widen the option space. The editorial job is still to choose, verify, sharpen, and take responsibility for every published word.

Define the job

Audience, action, channel, product truth, tone, and constraints.

Research the product

Source facts, edge cases, terminology, user questions, and reputation risks.

Generate with intent

Use structured prompts for angles, outlines, variants, and useful disagreement.

Edit like a human

Cut generic language, verify claims, restore voice, and strengthen rhythm.

Adapt and learn

Rebuild for each channel, then use audience feedback to improve the next brief.
